## Verdict

**Moving the terrain solve after retargeting is the right architecture.** Contact and reach must be solved on the rendered 17-bone rig, not inferred from the native skeleton. Render those solved transforms through the same bind matrices and LBS implementation used for validation. Preserve the native motion/features separately; label their coordinate space and timestamps so downstream code cannot mistake them for corrected contacts.

For speed and robustness, use a rigid sole proxy for planning, fixed-length analytical leg IK, and **actual skinned-geometry validation with rejection or replanning**.

## 1. Extract a usable sole footprint

Use rest-mesh geometry, skin weights, bone transforms, and inverse binds. Weights identify candidate foot regions; they do **not** identify the outsole reliably. Prefer sole vertex/triangle tags, or manually inspect a geometric extraction once per character.

For rest vertex \(x_v\), its foot-local coordinate is

\[
q_v=(G_f^{rest})^{-1}x_v,
\]

assuming mesh and bone transforms share a coordinate convention. Retain the sole’s 3D shape and a projected contact polygon; flattening everything loses toe curvature and heel offsets.

**Mixed foot/shin weights are the principal trap.** Under standard LBS,

\[
x_v(t)=\sum_j w_{vj}G_j(t)B_j^{-1}x_v.
\]

Consequently, \(G_f(t)^{-1}x_v(t)\) generally changes with ankle articulation. A rest-pose footprint is not necessarily a conservative runtime footprint.

Practical choices:

- Make outsole vertices effectively rigid to the foot, if asset editing is acceptable.
- Otherwise, build a foot-local envelope of deformed sole vertices over the permitted ankle/knee pose range. Use its projected hull for edge containment and its lowest surface for clearance.
- Sampling poses produces an empirical envelope, **not a proof**. Add a margin based on measured deformation, enforce joint limits, and still validate runtime LBS. A guaranteed envelope requires bounds over the permitted transform range.

A hull enclosing heel and toe also conservatively rejects unsupported gaps between them. That is preferable for an initial implementation.

## 2. Plan contacts on actual geometry

Represent each tread and connected deck as its actual support surface, with consistent seams and collision geometry matching the renderer. Treat risers, sides, undersides, and the moving door as obstacles—not a heightfield or hidden ramp.

Plan alternating **world-space sole poses**, including orientation. During stance, hold the sole pose fixed; convert it to a foot-bone target using the sole-to-bone offset. For moving supports, use support-local anchors.

Erode usable support regions by the footprint plus an initial **10–20 mm edge margin**. Connected coplanar deck pieces should act as one support region, avoiding false rejection at internal seams.

Use smooth swing paths with zero endpoint velocity. Start with **30–50 mm clearance above relevant obstacle tops**, but test the entire swept shoe against risers: ankle height alone does not establish clearance. Retime for the door using its actual trajectory; reject passages without clearance for the rest of the character.

## 3. Solve pelvis and legs

For thigh/shin lengths \(L_1,L_2\), constrain hip-to-ankle distance:

\[
|L_1-L_2|+\epsilon \le d \le L_1+L_2-\epsilon.
\]

Start with an extension reserve \(\epsilon\) of **1–2% of total leg length**. Account for rotated sole offsets when deriving ankle targets.

With fixed horizontal pelvis motion, derive feasible pelvis-height intervals from each active leg’s distance bounds. Joint limits and knee direction further restrict feasibility. Solve a short-horizon objective penalizing deviation from retargeted pelvis height, vertical velocity, and acceleration, subject to those constraints. Do not smooth afterward in a way that violates reach. Empty feasible sets require changing steps, timing, or pelvis horizontal motion—not stretching bones.

Use analytical two-bone IK. Construct knee poles from the retargeted bend direction, transported over time and biased toward a stable character-forward direction. Preserve the previous plane near straight-leg singularities; prevent sign flips. Validate joint limits explicitly.

## 4. Minimum rejection gates

The following are starting tolerances for a metre-scale character, not universal guarantees:

- **Contact:** stance sole gap within roughly **±2 mm**; anchor drift below **3 mm per stance**.
- **Reach:** no infeasible target; solved segment-length error below **0.1%**; no joint-limit violations.
- **Support:** footprint inside the usable support union with the chosen margin; reject heel/toe overhang unless intentionally supported.
- **Penetration:** validate all skinned sole vertices against rendered support, rejecting penetration beyond **2 mm**. Also test sole triangles/edges against obstacle triangles: vertices alone miss intersecting faces and narrow obstacles.
- **Between frames:** use swept collision or adaptive subdivision. An initial subdivision trigger is **2 mm maximum relevant surface-point displacement**, using rotational/deformation bounds, with a clearance margin covering that displacement. Sparse samples alone cannot certify clearance.
- **Segment joins:** check pose continuity, contact labels, anchor identity, velocities, and collision across the join. Initial limits: **2 mm position**, **0.5° orientation**, and **0.05 m/s velocity** mismatch. Repair/revalidate joins exceeding them.

## Is a rigid sole sufficient?

Often **yes for planning common stiff shoes**, but “mostly foot-weighted” is not sufficient evidence. Bound deviation by \(\sum_{j\ne f}w_j\|\Delta_j\|\), then measure actual outsole deformation over allowed poses. If it remains below your clearance budget—say **1–2 mm**—the proxy is useful. Keep full LBS validation regardless.
